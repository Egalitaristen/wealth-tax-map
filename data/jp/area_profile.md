## 2. Wealth tax history and live proposals (2010 onwards)
- **Historical background**: Japan enacted an annual net wealth tax (*Fuyūzei*, 富裕税) in 1950 following the Shoup Mission recommendation (*Shoup Report*, シャウプ勧告), levying an annual tax on individual net assets exceeding 5 million yen (at progressive rates of 0.5% to 3.0%). It was repealed in 1953 primarily due to difficulties in asset tracking prior to computerized banking, being replaced by interest/dividend income taxation and inheritance taxes.
- **2010–present revival**: With rising wealth concentration and the so-called "100-million-yen wall" (1億円の壁, where effective income tax rates drop for incomes exceeding 100 million yen due to the 20% flat capital gains tax rate), proposals for an annual recurrent net wealth tax (*富裕税*, *fuyūzei*) re-emerged in the National Diet, political parties, and civil society:
  - **Japanese Communist Party (JCP)**: Officially included an annual net wealth tax in its national election manifestos since the 2010s. The proposal levies a progressive annual rate between 0.5% and 3.0% on individuals whose net wealth exceeds 500 million yen (targeting roughly 1 in 1,000 households), with home residences and agricultural land exempt or assessed at inheritance tax valuations ([JCP 2024 Manifesto](https://www.jcp.or.jp/web_policy/11866.html)).
  - **Social Democratic Party (SDP / 社民党)**: In the 2025 national tax policy survey conducted by the Citizens' Liaison Committee for Fair Taxation, officially confirmed that it "positions strengthening taxation on the wealthy, including creating a wealth tax (asset taxation), as a key policy," introducing progressive taxation on net financial assets above a specified threshold ([Fair Tax Survey 2025](https://tax-justice.com/?p=1597)).
  - **Reiwa Shinsengumi (れいわ新選組)**: In the same 2025 survey, officially endorsed wealth tax creation: "After introducing a wealth tax, while discussion is needed on how to allocate its revenue, having the wealthy who have ability to pay bear their proper burden is appropriate from tax principles" ([Fair Tax Survey 2025](https://tax-justice.com/?p=1597)).
  - **Diet debates**: Formally debated in the House of Councillors Financial Affairs Committee (Akira Koike, 2025-03-13; Daimon Mikishi, 2022-03-16), House of Representatives Budget/Finance Committees (Akira Kasai, 2012-03-06; Toru Miyamoto, 2017-02-22; Banri Kaieda, 2021-02-26), and Councillors Plenary (Yasue Funayama, 2022-01-21).
  - **Civil society & academic mobilization (2025)**: Publication of 『なぜ資産に課税しないのか 富裕税入門――富の格差是正のために』 (*Why Not Tax Wealth? Introduction to Wealth Tax: For Correcting Wealth Inequality*, Akashi Shoten, August 2025), co-edited by former Nichibenren president Kenji Utsunomiya and Tokyo University Professor Emeritus Satoshi Daigo, alongside former Aoyama Gakuin President Yoshikazu Miki (proposing a 2% minimum asset tax), tax accountant Katsuhiko Kondo, US EA Motonan Narita, and economist Kan Goda.

## 4. Parties
- **Japanese Communist Party (JCP / 日本共産党)**: Core proponent of progressive annual net wealth tax (0.5%–3% above 500m yen net assets) in its official election platforms and Diet bills.
- **Social Democratic Party (SDP / 社会民主党)**: Committed to introducing a progressive net financial asset wealth tax (*富裕税* / *金融資産課税*).
- **Reiwa Shinsengumi (れいわ新選組)**: Officially endorsed introducing a wealth tax on high-net-worth individuals in written policy commitments to the Fair Tax Coalition.
- **Constitutional Democratic Party (CDP / 立憲民主党)**: Main centre-left opposition party. Stated in 2025 that while asset inequality must be corrected, it does not currently have concrete wealth tax legislation, focusing instead on inheritance/gift tax progressivity and financial income tax reform.
- **Democratic Party for the People (DPFP / 国民民主党)**: Prioritizes raising basic income exemptions; notes wealth tax requires further study.
- **Liberal Democratic Party (LDP / 自由民主党) & Komeito (公明党)**: Ruling coalition. Opposes net wealth tax, citing asset evaluation complexity, capital flight risks, and historical repeal in 1953.

## 5. Government below national level
- **Structure**: 47 Prefectures (都道府県) containing 1,749 Municipalities (市区町村: 23 special wards of Tokyo, 792 cities, 743 towns, 189 villages).
- **Taxing powers**: Local autonomy is guaranteed under Chapter 8 of the Constitution, but local tax items are strictly regulated by the Local Tax Act (地方税法, *Chihōzei-hō*). Local governments collect Fixed Assets Tax (固定資産税, *kotei shisanzei* on real estate and depreciable equipment) and Inhabitant Tax (住民税, *jūminzei* on personal/corporate income), but do not have statutory authority to enact a local net wealth tax on individuals without national statutory delegation.
- **Local council action**: Local municipal and prefectural assemblies regularly pass formal opinions / written petitions (意見書, *ikensho*) addressed to the Prime Minister and Diet under Article 99 of the Local Autonomy Act, calling for national tax policy changes.

## 6. Search terms
- **Core inclusion terms**:
  - `富裕税` (*fuyūzei* — wealth tax / tax on the wealthy)
  - `資産課税` (*shisankazei* — asset taxation)
  - `純資産税` (*junshisanzei* — net wealth tax)
  - `富裕層課税` (*fuyūsō kazei* — taxation on high-net-worth individuals)
  - `億万長者課税` (*okuman chōja kazei* — billionaire tax)
  - `財産税` (*zaisanzei* — property/capital tax; also refers historically to the 1946 one-off capital levy)
- **Exclusion terms (distinguish carefully)**:
  - `内部留保課税` (*naibu ryūho kazei* — tax on corporate retained earnings / internal reserves; exclude unless accompanied by personal wealth tax)
  - `金融所得課税` (*kin’yū shotoku kazei* — taxation on capital gains and dividends; exclude if only reforming income tax rate)
  - `固定資産税` (*kotei shisanzei* — standard local property/real estate tax)
  - `相続税` / `贈与税` (*sōzokuzei* / *zōyozei* — inheritance / gift taxes)
